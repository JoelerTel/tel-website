GFCI Outlet Testing Guide: How to Test, Reset, and When to Replace

By Texas Electric and Light — Licensed Electricians, TECL #28315

GFCI outlets — the ones with the Test and Reset buttons you find in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, and outdoor areas — are one of the most effective safety devices in your home. They can detect a dangerous electrical fault in as little as 1/40th of a second and cut power before it can cause serious injury or death.

The problem is that GFCI outlets fail. The sensors inside degrade over time, and a failed GFCI looks exactly like a working one — except it provides zero protection. Testing takes 30 seconds and should be done monthly.

What Does a GFCI Outlet Actually Do?

A standard outlet simply delivers power. A GFCI outlet constantly monitors the current flowing out to whatever is plugged in versus the current returning. Under normal conditions, these are equal. If current is leaking — flowing somewhere it shouldn't, like through a person — the GFCI detects the imbalance (as small as 5 milliamps) and cuts power in milliseconds.

This matters most in wet areas. Water conducts electricity, and bathrooms, kitchens, and outdoor areas are where electrical faults are most likely to interact with people. That is why GFCI protection is required by code in those locations.

Where Are GFCI Outlets Required in Austin Homes?

The National Electrical Code — adopted in Texas — requires GFCI protection in:

  • All bathroom outlets
  • Kitchen countertop outlets within 6 feet of a sink
  • Garage outlets
  • All outdoor outlets
  • Crawl spaces and unfinished basement areas
  • Near swimming pools, hot tubs, and spas
  • Boathouses and pool pump locations

Older Austin homes built before the late 1970s may not have GFCI protection in all required locations — the code requirements have expanded significantly over the decades. If your older home doesn't have GFCI outlets in bathrooms, kitchens, or the garage, talk to a licensed electrician about adding them.

How to Test a GFCI Outlet — Step by Step

Testing takes about 30 seconds and requires no tools.

  1. Plug a lamp or phone charger into the outlet so you can confirm power is on
  2. Press the "Test" button — the outlet should immediately lose power (your lamp should go off)
  3. Press the "Reset" button — power should return (your lamp should come back on)

If the outlet does not lose power when you press Test, or does not restore power when you press Reset, the GFCI has failed and needs to be replaced.

Important: One GFCI outlet can protect multiple outlets downstream on the same circuit. If you lose power in a bathroom outlet that doesn't have its own Test/Reset buttons, look for a GFCI outlet nearby — often in another bathroom or the garage — that may have tripped. Reset that one first.

How to Reset a Tripped GFCI

When a GFCI trips — cutting power because it detected a fault — here is how to reset it:

  1. Unplug everything from the outlet and any outlets it protects
  2. Wait 30 seconds
  3. Press the Reset button firmly — you should hear or feel a click
  4. Test the outlet to confirm power is restored

If the GFCI trips again immediately after resetting, something is still causing the fault. Do not keep resetting it. The GFCI is doing its job — protecting you from something. Unplug everything and try resetting once more. If it trips with nothing plugged in, the problem is in the wiring and you need a licensed electrician.

When Should You Replace a GFCI Outlet?

GFCI outlets typically last 10-15 years, though harsh conditions — outdoor exposure, high humidity — can shorten that lifespan. Replace a GFCI outlet if:

  • It fails the test (doesn't cut power when you press Test)
  • It won't reset after a fault
  • It is more than 15 years old
  • It has been exposed to flooding or significant moisture
  • You see physical damage — scorch marks, cracked cover, or broken buttons

Key Takeaways

  • Test GFCI outlets monthly — a failed GFCI looks normal but provides zero protection
  • GFCI outlets are required in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, and all outdoor areas
  • If a GFCI trips repeatedly, don't keep resetting it — something is causing the fault
  • Replace GFCI outlets older than 15 years or that fail the test
  • One GFCI can protect multiple outlets — look upstream if an outlet loses power without its own test button
